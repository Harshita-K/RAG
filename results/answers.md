**Pipeline 2 context:** 16: Recital (18); 258: Article 14 – Information to be provided ; 270: Article 19 – Notification obligation reg

**Answer 2:** Based on Recital (18) from Chunk 16, the GDPR does not apply to the processing of personal data by a natural person in purely personal or household activities. However, since the GDPR does apply to controllers or processors which provide the means for processing personal data for such personal or household activities, someone keeping a personal address book might still fall under the regulation if they are providing services for this purpose. Therefore, while the primary scope of the GDPR does not cover personal address books, there could be instances where it does apply depending on the specific circumstances.

### Q2 · Keyword trap / synonym
**Q:** Can a decision about me be made only by a computer algorithm?

**Pipeline 1 context:** 277: Article 22 – Automated individual decisi; 85: Recital (71); 88: Recitals (71)–(72)

**Answer 1:** Based on Recital (71) and Article 22, decisions based solely on automated processing and producing legal effects for the data subject, such as automatically refusing an online credit application, are generally prohibited. However, such automated decision-making can be allowed under specific conditions, as mentioned in Recital (71) and Article 22. These conditions include ensuring that suitable measures are in place to safeguard the data subject's rights and freedoms and legitimate interests, and that the processing does not involve special categories of personal data. **Pipeline 2 context:** 276: Article 22 – Automated individual decisi; 88: Recitals (71)–(72); 277: Article 22 – Automated individual decisi

**Answer 2:** Based on the information provided in [Chunk 276 | page 46], a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, cannot be made if it significantly affects the individual. There are exceptions where such decisions can be made, such as when they are necessary for entering into or performing a contract, or when authorized by laws that provide suitable measures to protect the individual's rights and freedoms. However, in these cases, the individual must still have certain rights, such as the right to obtain human intervention from the controller. [Chunk 276 | page 46]

**Pipeline 1 context:** 306: Article 34 – Communication of a personal; 304: Article 33 – Notification of a personal ; 303: Article 33 – Notification of a personal 

**Answer 1:** According to the provided context, a company does not need to tell affected users about a data breach if the personal data breach is "unlikely to result in a risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons" [Chunk 304]. Additionally, if the notification to the supervisory authority is not made within 72 hours, it must be accompanied by reasons for the delay [Chunk 303]. However, these exceptions do not pertain to informing the data subjects directly. Therefore, if the breach poses a risk, the company should inform the affected users [Chunk 304].

**Pipeline 2 context:** 306: Article 34 – Communication of a personal; 105: Recital (85); 305: Article 34 – Communication of a personal

**Answer 2:** According to Article 34 of the GDPR, a company does not need to tell affected users about a data breach if they have implemented appropriate technical and organizational protection measures. These measures include applying encryption to the personal data affected by the breach, rendering the personal data unintelligible to unauthorized individuals. This condition is detailed in [Chunk 305 | page 52].

### Q4 · Exception / corner case
**Q:** Is a controller exempt from informing people when it got their data from someone else and contacting them would take disproportionate effort?

**Pipeline 1 context:** 247: Article 12 – Transparent information, co; 74: Recital (63); 67: Recital (57)

**Answer 1:** Based on the provided context, if a controller gets personal data from another source and it becomes disproportionate to contact the data subject due to the complexity or volume of the data, the controller may either charge a reasonable fee or refuse to act on the request. This is mentioned in Recital (63) of the GDPR, as stated in [Chunk 74 | page 12]. However, the controller must still bear the burden of demonstrating that the request is manifestly unfounded or excessive. Therefore, there is no explicit exemption for controllers from informing people when they get their data from another source, but there is a condition where contacting the data subject might be disproportionate.

**Pipeline 2 context:** 270: Article 19 – Notification obligation reg; 258: Article 14 – Information to be provided ; 67: Recital (57)

**Answer 2:** Based on Recital (57) in Chunk 67, if the personal data do not permit the controller to identify a natural person, the controller is not obliged to acquire additional information solely for compliance with GDPR provisions. However, the passage also states that the controller should not refuse to provide additional information if it is given by the data subject. Therefore, while there is an exception for disproportionate effort, the core principle is that the controller should not refuse to provide information if requested. [Chunk 67]

**Pipeline 2 context:** 61: Recital (52); 239: Article 9 – Processing of special catego; 236: Article 9 – Processing of special catego

### Q8 · Counter-example (re-ranking hurts)
**Q:** Is processing allowed to save someone's life?

**Pipeline 1 context:** 48: Recital (46); 228: Article 6 – Lawfulness of processing; 93: Recital (77)

**Answer 1:** According to Recital (46) in Chunk 48, processing of personal data can be lawful if it is necessary to protect an interest which is essential for the life of the data subject or that of another natural person. However, Article 6 in Chunk 228 specifies that processing based on the vital interest of another natural person should in principle take place only where the processing cannot be manifestly based on another legal basis. Therefore, while saving someone's life is a valid reason for processing personal data, it must not be the only basis for such processing unless no other legal basis exists. [Chunk 48, Chunk 228]

**Pipeline 2 context:** 48: Recital (46); 59: Recital (51); 178: Recital (146)

**Answer 2:** According to Recital (46) in [Chunk 48], processing of personal data can be considered lawful if it is necessary to protect an interest which is essential for the life of the data subject or that of another natural person. However, processing based on the vital interest of another natural person should in principle take place only where the processing cannot be manifestly based on another legal basis. Therefore, while saving someone's life could justify processing under certain conditions, it must not be the sole reason and should not be the default option. [Chunk 48] and [Chunk 59] provide the relevant recitals.
